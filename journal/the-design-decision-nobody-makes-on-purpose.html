The most overlooked design decision on a golf course.

August 2026

A club will bring in a course architect to get a single green exactly right. The tee markers get picked off a catalogue page in an afternoon.

That is the gap. Almost everything on a course is designed on purpose, and you can read the intent in it. The tee markers, the yardage markers, the signage, and the plaques rarely get any of that thought. They are ordered, not designed, and a purchasing decision stands in for a design one.

It shows, because these are the objects a member is closest to. Not the turf, not the irrigation. The marker at the tee, the sign on the walk to the range, the plaque on the hole everyone remembers. A player reads a course through a hundred small signals, and these are among the few they meet up close, on every hole, all season. Get them right and the course feels deliberate. Leave them to the catalogue and it feels assembled.

This is where a club's character either carries outside or stops at the clubhouse door. Courses spend heavily to make the clubhouse feel like theirs, then let the first tee revert to whatever the supplier had in stock. A marker made for one course, in wood chosen for it and cut with its crest, does exactly the same job as a generic one and says the thing a generic one never can: someone here was paying attention.

Then there is whether it survives, which is the part we think about most. Ben has spent fifteen years working in wood, most of it learning how a given piece moves, checks, and weathers. That knowledge is the reason we do not finish these the way you would finish furniture. Wood that lives outside takes rain, snow, months of UV, and sprinkler water, and a hard-wax oil that is perfect on a dining table will not survive a winter on a tee box. So we use exterior- and marine-grade finishes instead, and we cut the detail into the wood by laser rather than laying paint or vinyl on top, because the top is where the weather starts.

Build it to survive and you have answered the cost question too, and the sustainability one with it. The cheapest marker is rarely the cheapest over ten years, because the real expense is the replacement cycle. A piece that lasts is not the one being binned and reordered every few seasons. That, not a badge on a spec sheet, is where the sustainability actually is.

None of this needs a big budget. It needs one decision made on purpose: what the course should feel like at the points a member meets it up close, and then the will to build those points to last.

Every course makes this decision. Most just let the catalogue make it for them.
